\subsection{Electromagnetically Induced Transparency}

In order to gain an intuition on electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT), we will start with a classical toy model of the atom. As is a common theme in physics, a hydrogenic atom in a time dependent electromagnetic field can be approximately modelled as a driven harmonic oscillator. So rather than dealing with quantized photons, we will consider a classical oscillating electromagnetic fields with frequencies nearly resonant with those of the atomic transitions.

To do this, a few assumptions will be made \cite{TODO}: first, the wavelengths of the light sources interacting with the atom have wavelengths much greater than the Bohr radius of the atom ($\lambda >> a_0$); second, the detunings of incoming beams are small in proportion to the transition frequencies considered. These two assumption happen to be consistent with one another.

The first assumption allows for us to approximate the electromagnetic field experienced by the atom as constant in space but varying in time since the change in electromagnetic field strength between the two ends the atom in the transverse wave direction is relatively small.

There exists the relation between the electric and magnetic field strengths of light which arises from Maxwell's equation \cite{TODO}
\begin{equation}
    B = \frac{E}{c}.
\end{equation}
The Lorentz force is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{F} = q \left(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B} \right)
\end{equation}
where $q$ is charge, and $\mathbf{v}$ is particle velocity. Using the Bohr model for approximation, we have that quantized electron velocities for a hydrogenic atom have magnitude
\begin{equation}
    v_n = \alpha \frac{Z}{n} c
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the fine structure constant, $Z$ is the atomic number, and $n$ is the principle quantum number \cite{Atomic book p.23}. We can therefore see by rough approximation that we have
\begin{equation}
    |\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}| \lessapprox \alpha Z c \frac{E}{c} = \alpha Z E.
\end{equation}
We are considering Rubidium with atomic number $Z = 37$, but this neglects the effects of screening caused by the electrons in inner shells of the atom. Electron position is distributed in a cloud about the atom for any orbital, though we can expect the $36$ electrons of inner shells between the valence electron (singular in its shell) and the nuclear charge of $37e$ to roughly cancel $36e$ of charge such that the valence electron experiences about $1e$. By this line of reasoning we have
\begin{equation}
    |\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}| \lessapprox \alpha E.
\end{equation}
Thus, the force due to the external magnetic field is about $1/137$ that of the external electric field. So we may neglect the external magnetic field for simplification.
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{F} \approx q \mathbf{E}
\end{equation}

All of these approximations can be justified from the perspective that this model is a first estimate for system behavior which will be refined and iterated upon.

This brings us to a physicist's favorite abstraction: the harmonic oscillator. The potential of which the valence electron experiences can be expanded in a Taylor series and approximated as quadratic \cite{TODO}. The mass of the nucleus is much greater than the electron mass, so we may approximate the nucleus to be fixed. So we have a mass with some equilibrium distance from a fixed point under a quadratic potential, i.e. a simple harmonic oscillator. We may then add in our external time-dependent driving force to obtain a driven harmonic oscillator.

When a charge oscillates, it emits photons as its movement produces a changing electric and magnetic field. So when our system is driven on resonance, then an electromagnetic field of that same frequency will be produced. Here lies the idea behind electromagnetically induced transparency. By driving a cloud of these hydrogenic atoms on resonance, the field essentially `passes through' the medium.

\subsection{Structure of atomic spectra}

Atomic states for the electron of a hydrogenic atom are enumerated by the quantum numbers $n$ (principle quantum number), $l$ (angular momentum quantum number), $m_l$ (magnetic quantum number), and $m_s$ (spin quantum number)~\cite{TODO Macyntyre}.

\begin{equation}
    n \in \mathbb{N},\;\; l \in \{0,1,\ldots,n-1\},\;\; m \in \{-l, \ldots, l\}
\end{equation}

The energy levels for an unperturbed hydrogenic atom modelled with just the coulomb potential are quite simple as they just depend on the principal quantum number.

\begin{equation}\label{unperturbed}
    E_n = - \frac{(Z \alpha)^2 mc^2}{2n^2} = - \frac{Z^2 Ry}{n^2}
\end{equation}
where $Z$ is the atomic number, $\alpha$ is the fine structure constant, $m$ is the electron mass, and $c$ is the speed of light \cite{TODO Tong}. These unperturbed energy levels each have degeneracy of $n^2$.

The real picture, is more complicated, however. There are fine structure and hyperfine structure perturbations on these energy levels, splitting energy levels and decreasing their degeneracies.

\subsubsection{Fine Structure Corrections}

Fine structure corrections arise due to relativistic corrections to the non-relativistic Sch\"{o}dinger's equation. In particular, we have a pure relativistic correction from considering electron momentum (which is a non-negligible fraction of the speed of light), a spin-orbit coupling correction, and the Darwin correction. To first order perturbation theory, the combined energy shift of these contributions is 

\begin{equation}
    \Delta E_{fs} = \frac{(Z \alpha)^4}{2} \frac{mc^2}{n^3}\left( \frac{3}{4n} - \frac{2}{2j+1} \right)
\end{equation}
where $j = |l \pm \frac{1}{2}|$ \cite{TODO Tong}. Notice that these perturbations are scaled by a factor of $(Z \alpha)^2$ in comparison to Eq.~\eqref{unperturbed} such that for atoms with a sufficiently large atomic number, these approximate energy shifts arrived at by perturbation theory break down.

\subsubsection{Hyperfine Structure Corrections}

There exist yet smaller energy shifts due to the interaction between the internal magnetic fields of the nucleus and the orbital motion of the electron along with its spin \cite{TODO Macyntyre p.355}. For our purposes, it is sufficient to consider only hyperfine splitting for $l=0$ states, which have the correction term

\begin{scriptsize}
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta E_{hfs} = \frac{2}{3} \frac{m}{M} (Z \alpha)^4 \frac{mc^2}{n^3} \left[ F(F+1) - s(s+1) - I(I+1) \right]
    \end{equation}
\end{scriptsize}
where $I$ is the nuclear spin quantum number, $s$ is the electron spin quantum number, and $F$ is the total angular momentum quantum number with operator $\haf F = \hat I + \hat S$ \cite{TODO Macyntyre p. 369 and Tong p. 82}.

\subsection{Atomic transitions}

In a hydrogenic atom, the transitions between states are mediated by excitations in the electromagnetic field (i.e.~photons). It is crucial in atomic physics to understand the probabilities of these atomic state transitions.

The interaction between an atom and the electromagnetic field is not a linear interaction, creating difficulty in finding an exact solution. To obtain approximate behavior, first order perturbation theory can be applied to dominating effects of this interaction~\cite{TODO atomic book}. From this, one obtains the transition dipole matrix with elements
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{\mu}_{j,i} = \bra{j}\hat{\mathbf{d}}\ket{i} = e\bra{j}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\ket{i}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{d}} = e\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is the dipole transition operator and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is the position operator~\cite{TODO atomic book}.

If $\mathbf{\mu}_{j,i} = 0$, then the transition between states $\ket{i}$ and $\ket{j}$ is a `forbidden' dipole transition, otherwise the transition is an allowed dipole transition. This criterion enforces the dipole selection rules that
\begin{equation}
    \Delta l = \pm 1\\
    \Delta m_l = 0, \pm 1.
\end{equation}

It is important to note that these transition rules are not always perfectly enforced. Since perturbation theory was applied to obtain them, there exist transitions which disobey these rules, however, these transitions occur with significantly decreased probability.

\subsection{Three-Level Atom}

In order to meaningfully utilize the power of quantum information via quantum states, it is beneficial to work with simplified, yet somewhat abstract, systems. It would be ideal if we could realize any imaginary quantum systems that exactly match our mathematical descriptions. Reality is not so convenient, however. We must work within the constraints enforced by the available quantum systems in terms of allowed states and transitions between those states.

With enough ingenuity, we can use existing systems to approximate useful mathematical abstractions. Enter, the three-level (hydrogenic) atom. All hydrogenic atoms, in theory, have infinitely many states up to ionization ($n \in \mathbb{N}$), and transition probabilities between many of these states can be non-zero. Though by finding a subset of states with desired characteristics and leveraging transition rules where any undesired state transitions are suppressed, a physical atom can be used to approximate a model system. This becomes especially useful in the limit of a large number of atoms, where statistically the desired transitions are the most probable, and any undesired transitions are vastly outnumbered, giving approximately the desired behavior.

A three-level atom has three distinct states with some set of allowed transitions between those states. The transition amplitude between states $\ket{1}$ and $\ket{2}$ is suppressed, so if the system is in state $\ket{2}$, then in order to transition back to state $\ket{1}$ the system will likely need to transition through the excited state $\ket{3}$. This makes $\ket{2}$ a quasi-stable state.

If there were a way to suppress all transitions to state $\ket{3}$, then we would obtain a system in which the atom is bound to states $\ket{1}$ and $\ket{2}$ such that any photons which would stimulate a transition to the excited state cannot be absorbed.

There is, in fact, a way to do just this. By driving the system with external electromagnetic fields (lasers) at the transition frequencies, destructive quantum interference between the two fields along with specific initial conditions results in zero probability for the system to be in the excited state \cite{TODO - Find ref}. $\ket{3}$ is then called a `dark state'. In practice, since perfect precision in real-world systems is difficult, both lasers are detuned from the exact transition frequency. The probe laser stimulates transition 13 $\omega_p = \omega_{13} + \Delta$, $\Delta << \omega_{13}$, and the control laser stimulates transition 23 $\omega_c = \omega_{23} + \delta$, $\delta << \omega_{23}$ (see Fig.~\ref{ThreeLevelAtom}).

The main idea is that the system then becomes effectively `transparent' to the probe and control laser frequencies. This is how EIT can be produced in practice.

To understand how this would be useful in practice, suppose you have a cloud of rubidium atoms at some temperature $T$ (high enough such that they are in a gaseous state, say around $300K$ to $400 K$) in the absence of any external electromagnetic fields (i.e.~no Stark or Zeeman effects), then by the principles of statistical mechanics, the ground state is the most common state. So the majority of atoms will sit in one of the two long-lived states ($\ket{1}$ or $\ket{2}$), while a smaller proportion of atoms are in the excited state $\ket{3}$. If the probe and control lasers are then powered on, we should get an approximate dark state where the majority of atoms are in states $\ket{1}$ or $\ket{3}$ while hardly any sit in $\ket{3}$. There may exist some atoms in higher excited states, but at a negligible population. This cloud of atoms will then exhibit EIT.


\subsection{Cavity enhanced EIT}

Consider a system composed of Avogadro's number of three-level atoms sitting. In the ideal scenario, all atoms would have the exact same transition frequencies such that when the probe and control beams are fired through the cloud, the frequency of transparency is exact. In reality, this is not so. The system needs to be in a gaseous excited state where the electromagnetic fields of nearby atoms interact, inducing Stark and Zeeman shifts in their spectra, and Doppler shifts in the observed frequency of light would occur as some atoms would be moving away from the laser while some would be moving towards it. In addition, the laser used will have some bandwidth of emission frequencies. These effects combine to cause spectral broadening in the transparency window allowed by EIT.

Before moving on, we need an important fact. When light travels through some material with complex electric susceptibility (i.e. complex index of refraction), then the light will receive some phase shift due to the real-valued component of susceptibility $\chi_R$ and some decay due to the imaginary-valued component $\chi_I$, such that the oscillating electric field will propagate as \cite{TODO - Optics textbook}
\begin{equation}\label{Beer}
    E = E_0 e^{i(k z - \omega t)}e^{i\chi_R k z / 2}e^{-\chi_I k z / 2}
\end{equation}
where the rate of decay then grows exponentially with distance traveled $z$. The decay term $e^{-\chi_I k z / 2}$ is known as Beer-Lambert law \cite{TODO - Optics textbook}.

Consider placing the atomic gas in an interferometric cavity (such as a Fabry-Perot cavity), the transparency window can be greatly narrowed. This can be though of classically or quantum mechanically: in a classical interpretation, Beer's law (Eq.~\eqref{Beer}) will cause any frequencies which are slightly off resonant will decay after many reflections within the cavity (the path length is effectively longer); in a quantum interpretation, non-resonant states of light will destructively interfere within the cavity, leading to a decreased probability that they propagate.

\subsection{Squeezed States}

According to Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, the product of the standard deviations of two canonically conjugate observables, $x$ and $p$, must satisfy

\begin{equation}
    \Delta x \Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}.
\end{equation}

For light of sufficient intensity, one can consider its amplitude and phase as canonically conjugate variables~\cite{TODO}. For a generically produced beam, one would expect the standard deviations of  amplitude and phaseto to be roughly equal. If measurement were to be performed in a single quadrature (amplitude for example), then reducing the uncertainty in that quadrature would result in a more percise overall reading.

This leads to the idea of a squeezed state in which one quadrature has its uncertainty decreased below the shot noise level at the expense of the other, such that the uncertainty principle is still observed. It has been shown that optical squeezed states can be produced through a FWM process in optical cavities \cite{Slusher}.


\subsection{Continuous Variable Quantum Information}

If multiple beams are entangled such that a measurement of one of these variables on one beam yields information about the other beams, then we have what is called continuous variable entanglement~\cite{TODO}. In this way, quantum information can be stored, processed, and transmitted using continuous beams rather than with individual photons.


\subsection{How Sub-Poissonian Light and Photon Anti-Bunching Effects Affect Continuous Variable Measuements}

Continuous variable measurements allow for small quantum fluctuations to be less significant in measurement procedures when compared to individual photon entanglement, though there still exist challenges in performing measurements. Continuous variable measurements are typically performed with homodyne detectors~\cite{TODO}, a type of interferometer which compares some input beam against a controlled probe beam and takes the difference between their readings~\cite{TODO}.

Light is quantized and beam intensity is not that high. Individual photons arrive at the detector in a Poisson process, resulting in shot noise for the detector~\cite{TODO}. Due to this, the reading of the input beam can be swayed by individual photon arrival events. It would be highly desirable for the input beam to follow sub-Poissonian statistics (with the same mean as a Poisson distribution but a lower variance)~\cite{TODO} and for the beam to display photon anti-bunching (arrival events are distributed more evenly as opposed to having intensity spikes)~\cite{TODO}.

It has been shown that applying cavity EIT to three-level atoms can produce sub-Poissonian light~\cite{Souza}, and that applying FWM to three-level systems can produce photon anti-bunching effects~\cite{Du}. Combining these techniques theoretically allows for the information of continuous variable beams to be more reliably transmitted to some measurement device, such that readings fall below the shot-noise level.

\section{Janyes-Cummings Model}

Before moving on, it is useful to look at another simplified model, though one with very different characteristics and description. We may then combine the ideas from these models to form more complex systems.

Introducing the Jaynes-Cummings model. For this, we take a step back to consider a two-level atom, but add complexity by placing the atom within a resonant cavity (see Fig.~\ref{Jaynes-Cummings})~\cite{TODO Tong?}. The field of a single photon within the cavity is quite significant as it will bounce back and forth between cavity walls, building up a non-negligible field. So we must quantize photons in the Fock basis. The cavity is finely tuned to a resonant frequency $\omega_c$ such that any other frequency of light destructively interferes with itself. This leads to a simplification for our Fock basis which may be restricted to photons of a single frequency. There can be a detuning between the frequencies, but we will consider the case of perfect resonance here $\omega = \omega_a = \omega_c$.


\begin{figure}%[h!]
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\columnwidth]{Jaynes-Cummings.png}
    \caption{Depiction of the system for a Jaynes-Cummings model with (a) a resonant cavity of frequency $\omega_c$ containing (b) a two-level atom of transition frequency $\omega_a$.}
    \label{Jaynes-Cummings}
\end{figure}


The states of our system live within the combined Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_{atom} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{photon}$, and so states are described by $\ket{atom} \otimes \ket{photons}$ where $\ket{atom}$ has two states and $\ket{photon}$ describes all $n$-photon states. Consider a state containing $N$ photons worth of energy. Then the possible states of the system are $\ket{\downarrow} \otimes \ket{N}$ and $\ket{\uparrow} \otimes \ket{N-1}$.

The Hamiltonian describing this system is given by \cite{TODO - Tong?} \cite{Scala}
\begin{equation}
    \hat H_{JC} = \hat H_{Atom} + \hat H_{Photon} + \hat H_{Interaction}.
\end{equation}
The atomic term is given by
\begin{align}
    \hat H_{Atom} &= \frac{\hbar \omega}{2} \hat \sigma_z \nonumber\\
    &= \frac{\hbar \omega}{2} \left(\begin{array}{cc}
        1 & 0\\
        0 & -1
    \end{array}\right).
\end{align}
The photonic term is given by
\begin{equation*}
    \hat H_{Photon} = \hbar \omega\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger} \hat{a} + \frac{1}{2}\right).
\end{equation*}
where we may shift by the zero-point energy since it is a constant term to obtain 
\begin{equation*}
    \hat H_{Photon} = \hbar \omega \hat{a}^{\dagger} \hat{a}
\end{equation*}
Then finally, the interaction term is given by
\begin{align}
    \hat H_{Interaction} &= \frac{\hbar \Omega}{2} \left( \hat{\sigma}_+ \otimes \hat{a} + \hat{\sigma}_- \otimes \hat{a}^{\dagger} \right) \nonumber\\
    &= \frac{\hbar \Omega}{2} \left(\begin{array}{cc}
        0 & \hat{a}\\
        \hat{a}^{\dagger} & 0
    \end{array}\right)
\end{align}
where $\Omega$ is the Rabi frequency and $\hat{\sigma}_{\pm}$ are the atomic raising and lowering operators.

In order to combine the Hilbert spaces for these two separate quantizes systems, block-operator notation is applied. Notice that $\hat H_{Atom}$ acts on the two-state atomic basis, while $\hat H_{Photon}$ acts on the infinite Fock basis of photons. We then must combine the two bases in $\hat H_{Interaction}$ which describes the creation of a photon in the Fock basis upon atomic emission, and the annihilation of a photon upon absorption. So this Hamiltonian should not be thought of as a two-by-two matrix acting on the atomic basis, but rather an infinite matrix enumerating the interactions between all possible states. All together, the final Hamiltonian is then~\cite{Scala}

\begin{align}
    \hat{H}_{JC} &= \frac{\hbar \omega}{2} \hat \sigma_z + \hbar \omega \hat{a}^{\dagger} \hat{a} + \frac{\hbar \Omega}{2} \left( \hat{\sigma}_+ \otimes \hat{a} + \hat{\sigma}_- \otimes \hat{a}^{\dagger} \right) \nonumber \\
    &= \frac{\hbar}{2} \left(\begin{array}{cc}
        \omega & \Omega \hat{a}\\
        \Omega \hat{a}^{\dagger} & -\omega
    \end{array}\right) + \hbar \omega \hat{a}^{\dagger} \hat{a}
\end{align}

This is not the whole story, however. Any real cavity has some photonic decay rate, meaning we must include dissipative operators and apply Lindbladian dynamics to solve for a steady state via the master equation. Our dissipative operator then becomes $L = \sqrt{\gamma} \hat{a}$ where $\gamma$ gives the decay rate such that our master equation is \cite{Scala}
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\rho} = - i \left[\hat{H}_{JC}, \rho\right] + \gamma \left( \hat{a} \rho \hat{a}^{\dagger} - \frac{1}{2} \hat{a}^{\dagger} \hat{a} \rho - \frac{1}{2} \rho \hat{a}^{\dagger} \hat{a} \right).
\end{equation}

This code differs slightly from the above description in that the atomic Hamiltonian basis is shifted such that it is in the rotating frame $\hat{H}_{Atom} = \hbar \omega \ket{e}\bra{e}$.
